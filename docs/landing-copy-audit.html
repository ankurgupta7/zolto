01 Why it feels heavier than it counts

1,380 words is a lot for a homepage, but it is not absurd on its own. What makes it tiring is the format it is delivered in. The reel is eight posts, and a post is exactly one screen — snap scrolling, one flick per chapter, as documented at the top of Landing.tsx.

On an ordinary scrolling page, extra words are cheap: the eye skips them and the reader stays in motion. A snap reel removes that escape hatch. Every post is a full stop the reader has to clear before earning the next flick, so the page collects eight forced reading stops instead of one long scroll a reader can triage. The same word count costs more here than it would anywhere else on the site. That is why it reads as tiring even to someone who reads a lot.

The second thing the numbers show: the page is trying to win an argument rather than earn a click. Five of the eight chapters open with an objection and answer it — the competitors have holes, the free tier is genuinely free, the pledge is sincere, the infrastructure list includes the bad parts, AI search is real. Every one of those arguments already has a page built for it: /pricing, /compare, /why-gwinn, /sovereignty and the launch diary. The homepage's job is to state the claim well enough that one of those links gets clicked.

The page already contains its own answer

The best band on this page, per word, is the cost strip in chapter five: two numbers, an arrow, 24 words of supporting text, and the argument is over. Nobody has to read it — they see it.

Three chapters earlier, the same page spends 172 prose words making a comparison that is structurally identical — three options, two properties, one winner — and it makes it in three columns of running text. That gap is the whole audit. The rest of this document applies the cost strip's method to the other seven chapters.

03 Four moves

Every proposal below is one of these. Naming them makes the edits arguable one at a time instead of as a wholesale rewrite.

Cut

Say it once. Most long paragraphs here make a point and then defend it against an objection the reader has not raised yet.

Table

Parallel prose is a table someone forgot to draw. Three options described in three paragraphs is a matrix.

Caption

Where a figure already exists, the paragraph beside it is usually narrating it. Shrink the words to a label on the drawing.

Defer

Move the long version to the page built for it. The links are already in the nav; the homepage only has to make them worth clicking.

04 The rewrites

Mockups are rough — real faces are Cormorant Garamond, Caveat and Inter; these stand in with the nearest system equivalents. The palette and the proportions are the real ones. Counts on each pair are every word in the panel — eyebrows, headings, labels and links included — measured the same way as the page itself, so before and after are like for like.

A  ·  The squeeze  ·  chapter 2 Table 197 → 62 −69%

The single biggest win on the page. Three cards, each a short paragraph, each describing a product by what it lacks — with a 42-word run-up and a 26-word summary that restates all three. Read at 1440px the cards are narrow columns of nine-word lines, which is the most tiring shape text can take.

It is a comparison of three options across two properties. Draw it and the argument is over before anyone starts reading — and the summary sentence at the bottom becomes unnecessary, because the reader can see that only one row has two ticks.

Keeps every fact, both citations and the ordering. What it drops is the narration of the comparison. On a phone the matrix is also better behaved than three cards: it is one panel instead of a sideways swipe.

B  ·  Free in person  ·  chapter 3 Cut Defer 120 → 42 −65%

The left card carries 110 words; the ticklist beside it says the same thing in a form nobody has to read. The second paragraph — 67 words, the longest block on the homepage — is a payment-processing disclaimer. It is honest and it should exist, but it belongs on /pricing, which already restates the pledge via PRICING_PROMISE.restatedByPricingFeeSection.

Cut here, moved to /pricing “Whoever processes your card and TWINT payments still charges their own rate, the same as they would anywhere else; that bill is between you and them, and we don't take a slice of it.”

The four ticks in the panel beside it already carry “photos, names, prices, stock counts” and “syncs to your website”, so the first paragraph was saying it twice on one screen.

C  ·  Trust  ·  chapter 6 Caption Table 219 → 133 −39%

The ledger is the right idea already — nine rows, a badge each, the embarrassing ones included. Two things weigh it down. The 68-word intro beside it explains a table that explains itself, and each row carries a full sentence of detail where a phrase would do (90 words of detail across nine cells, in the smallest type on the page).

I would not hide the detail — it is the credibility. Cut each cell to a phrase and add a residency bar above the table, so the shape of the answer lands before any row is read.

D  ·  What's coming  ·  chapter 7 Cut Caption 241 → 121 −50%

The wordiest chapter, and the only one carrying three separate arguments: a product claim (“Sell first. Sort it out later.”), a market thesis (“Your next customer is an AI.”) and a walkthrough of a market day. Three claims on one post is why it needs 241 words.

  • “Sell first. Sort it out later.” (39 words) is a product claim about the AI drafting listings — the same claim chapter 4's photo→listing panel already makes, with a drawing. Move the headline there and cut the paragraph.
  • The thesis paragraph (54 words) narrates the chart next to it. The chart shows two lines crossing; a paragraph saying two lines cross is the definition of a redundant caption.
  • The day-in-the-life steps average 20 words each beside icons that already show the action. Imperative fragments do it in eight.
E  ·  The two paragraphs worth keeping Cut 104 → 63 −39%

The hero body and the pledge are the only long paragraphs on the page that earn their length. Neither should be cut to a fragment — but both are currently one sentence longer than they need to be.

“TWINT, a card or cash on the phone or tablet you already own” is the clause that goes: the three sovereignty badges below already say TWINT BUILT IN, and the video beside it shows the phone. A hero paragraph should not carry a spec list.

“we're here to help you keep what you earn, and that's not changing” is the third restatement of the same promise in one quotation. Cutting it makes “we already have enough money” the last thing before the signature, which is the line that lands.

05 Three more things to draw

Beyond the rewrites above — places where a figure would replace prose that is currently doing a diagram's job.

An eight-dot rail with real labels

The reel already has a chapter rail down the right edge, but its labels only appear for the active post. Turning it into a persistent eight-word outline (Promise · Squeeze · Free · How · Cost · Trust · Coming · Start) tells a reader where the argument goes without them having to read it to find out. Readers tolerate more text when they can see how much is left.

The free plan as a receipt

Chapter 3's claim is fundamentally “the invoice says zero.” Drawing an actual monthly statement — line items for POS, storefront, inventory, all at CHF 0.00, with a total rule — makes the claim in a form that is unarguable, and it removes the need to say “forever”, “no trial clock” and “no starter tier” in words. Fits the existing illustration style in MarketingIllustrations.tsx.

Photo → listing as one drawn frame, not a paragraph

The PhotoToListing figure already shows a phone photo turning into a titled, priced, described listing. The 31-word paragraph above it describes that transformation in words first. Add the four languages as small flags or codes inside the drawing and the paragraph becomes a caption: “Photograph a piece. Publish the listing it writes.” — nine words.

06 What it cost to land

A was the largest single cut, as predicted — 137 words, and it needed no copywriting judgement because the facts were already written and only had to be arranged in a grid. It also bought back a swipe: the chapter is one slide now instead of two.

The long-form copy did not go anywhere. Every cut is a bodyShort / todayShort variant the reel uses; llms.txt, the SEO descriptions, the MCP briefs and the dedicated pages still render the full text. For a product whose thesis is being legible to a machine reading the page, gutting the machine-readable version to tidy the human one would have been a strange trade.

The one proposal that was wrong

Cutting “point-of-sale” and “phone” from the hero paragraph saved four words and would have cost the one sentence on the site that says what Gwinn is, in the word a buyer would search and an assistant would match on. A test caught it. The hero is 72 words rather than the 66 this document proposed, and that is the right number.

Each rewrite landed in five places — en, de, fr, it and shared/platform.ts — and the whole change is 3,270 passing tests, a clean typecheck, and every reel panel still fitting one screen at 375, 393, 768, 1280 and 1440.
